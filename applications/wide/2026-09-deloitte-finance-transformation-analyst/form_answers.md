# Deloitte application form, the four required answers

Deloitte replaces the cover letter with these four questions, 1440 characters each. Unlike `cover_letter.md`, this file is hand-written prose rather than output of `make_letter.py`, because there is no pipeline for form answers. Every claim in it traces to `content_base.yaml`. Paste each answer into its field as it is.

## 1. Describe a specific experience or situation that sparked your motivation to apply for our Finance Consultancy Programme. How do you see this programme at Deloitte helping you build on that experience?

The spark came at OSPA, where I worked in real estate product development and produced the initial investment analysis behind more than fifteen feasibility studies, sizing investment and return for each candidate site against buildable area, unit mix, price per square metre and parking. Those figures decided whether a project went ahead at all, and I found myself more curious about how they were built, defended and revised than about the drawings they supported. Wanting to understand that side of a business properly has shaped my work ever since. At Ramboll I worked with corporate clients who now have to evidence non-financial performance to something close to the standard demanded of their financial reporting, and at Sweco I built a pricing tool that parametrised expert fee bands and project economics so that bid decisions rested on figures. Smaller things point the same way, such as the Excel and VBA tool I made for a private client that ingests monthly statements, categorises transactions automatically and renders them into a dashboard he now decides on. Your programme builds deliberately what I have been assembling piece by piece, since the Finance Transformation bootcamp puts a proper foundation under the finance craftsmanship, and the first years staff an analyst across operational finance, enterprise performance management and ERP-enabled transformations rather than in one corner of the CFO function.

## 2. Please provide an example where you turned a challenging situation into a positive outcome. What did you learn from the experience?

At Isay Weinfeld Architects, bespoke staircases were consuming several days of modelling on every project, which limited how many options a team could test before committing to one. I wrote a Grasshopper plugin in Python that generates them directly from a floor plan and brought that task down to roughly twenty minutes. The harder part came afterwards. My colleagues had been doing the work by hand for years and had no particular reason to trust a tool built by someone sitting two desks away, and a plugin nobody opens saves nothing at all. So I sat with them to establish what they actually needed rather than what I had assumed, rebuilt parts of it around that, presented it in a studio workshop and then supported it project by project until it had been used on more than ten. What I took from it is that the technical work is the smaller half of any improvement, and that adoption is not a phase that happens after delivery but the thing delivery is for. It changed how I start this kind of work, because I now spend far more time on the requirements and on the people who will live with the result than on the build itself.

## 3. What personal qualities or values do you believe will help you succeed in the Finance Consultancy Programme at Deloitte, and how have these been demonstrated in your past experiences?

Two qualities have carried most of my work so far. The first is a preference for structuring a problem rather than describing it. At Ramboll I turned broad and uneven human rights evidence into an automated risk assessment system that consolidated supply chain data into a dashboard and updated salient risks dynamically, and I mapped the supply chains of more than twenty critical raw materials so that regulatory pressure became a concrete list of prospective clients rather than a theme. The second is being comfortable with people who are under no obligation to agree with me. Running my own practice for several years meant acquiring clients, scoping and pricing the work, and absorbing the cost myself whenever I scoped it optimistically, which made me precise about what I promise. At Sweco I sat with national water agencies and government counterparts in Baku to align on rehabilitation priorities and to get the technical data the project needed, and at OSPA I presented feasibility outcomes to developer clients deciding whether to buy a site. Both matter in a programme that staffs analysts across changing clients and disciplines, where the analysis only counts once someone outside my own team has understood it well enough to act on it.

## 4. Imagine you are given a blank canvas to design your ideal workday. What does it look like?

It would start early and quietly, with two or three uninterrupted hours on the analytical part of whatever project I am on, since that is when I do my best work on a model, a dataset or a document that has to be built rather than reviewed. The middle of the day would belong to other people. Ideally that means a session with a client where something is actually decided rather than reported on, and an hour with a colleague, either learning a part of the business I do not know yet or explaining a piece of what I have built. I would want at least one thing to leave my hands before the day ends, a version of a model, a draft recommendation or a working prototype, because work that stays open indefinitely loses its momentum and mine with it. The later afternoon would go to the parts nobody sees, cleaning up an analysis so a colleague can pick it up without me, writing down what changed and why, and preparing properly for the next conversation. The day would finish at a reasonable hour, and I would know exactly what the first task of the following morning is.
