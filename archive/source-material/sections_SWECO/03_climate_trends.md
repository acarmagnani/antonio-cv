# Observed Climate Trends

The data presented in Section [X.2] reflects the average climatic conditions for the 1991–2020 period, which serves as the baseline for this assessment. However, this 30-year baseline already incorporates significant climate change signals when compared to earlier decades. Figure [X].2 and Figure [X].3 present annual trends in mean surface air temperature and precipitation for the Absheron Peninsula over the longer historical record (approximately 1950–2024), based on data from the World Bank Climate Change Knowledge Portal (CRU TS v4.06).

## Temperature Trends


**Figure [X].2:** Annual Mean Surface Air Temperature Trend, 1950–2024; Absheron Peninsula, Azerbaijan

![Annual mean temperature trend, Absheron](data/graphs/Absheron/Trend avg temp Absheron.jpeg)


Figure [X].2 shows a clear upward trend in annual mean surface air temperature over the observational record for the Absheron Peninsula. Interannual variability is substantial, with individual years departing by ±1°C or more from the smoothed series; however, the underlying trend is unambiguous. The 5-year Gaussian-smoothed mean temperature increased from approximately **13.4°C in the early 1950s** to approximately **15.0°C by 2020**, representing a warming of approximately **1.6°C over 70 years**. Decadal averages confirm this trajectory: the mean annual temperature for the 1951–1960 decade was approximately 13.4°C, compared to approximately 14.7°C for the 2011–2020 decade — an increase of approximately 1.3°C over 60 years.

The warming trend is most pronounced from the mid-1990s onwards. Prior to approximately 1995, annual temperatures largely fluctuated around a relatively stable mean of 13.0–14.0°C; thereafter, the baseline shifted upward, with the majority of years from 2000 onwards recording annual mean temperatures in the range 13.8–15.5°C. The warmest years on record for the Absheron Peninsula fall within the most recent decade, with 2010 (15.5°C), 2023 (15.5°C), and 2019 (15.1°C) representing the highest annual means in the observational record. This pattern is consistent with the broader warming signal documented across the Caspian region and Central Asia, and is attributable to the global increase in greenhouse gas concentrations.

## Precipitation Trends


**Figure [X].3:** Annual Precipitation Trend, 1950–2024; Absheron Peninsula, Azerbaijan

![Annual precipitation trend, Absheron](data/graphs/Absheron/Trend precipitation Absheron.jpeg)


In contrast to the temperature record, Figure [X].3 does not show a clear directional trend in annual precipitation for the Absheron Peninsula. The record is instead characterised by **high interannual variability**, with annual totals ranging between approximately 230 mm and 650 mm over the observational period. This level of variability — equivalent to more than a factor of two between the driest and wettest years — is typical of semi-arid climates where precipitation is highly sensitive to the track and frequency of synoptic weather systems.

The 5-year Gaussian-smoothed series shows modest multi-decadal oscillations rather than a consistent long-term trend. The smoothed precipitation was approximately 300 mm in the early 1950s, rose to a peak of approximately 350 mm during the 1960s and early 1970s, and subsequently declined towards the 1990s. A partial recovery occurred in the 2000s, but the most recent years (2015–2020) show a return to below-average values, with the 2011–2020 decadal mean (approximately 289 mm) somewhat lower than the 1951–1960 mean (approximately 316 mm). While this recent drying signal may be consistent with projected trends towards decreasing precipitation in the region, the high interannual variability means that the observed change over the historical record is not statistically distinguishable from natural climate variability.

## Summary of Observed Trends

The observational record for the Absheron Peninsula reveals a **robust warming trend in surface air temperature** of approximately 1.6°C over the period 1950–2020, with the rate of warming accelerating from the mid-1990s onwards, and all recent years above the long-term mean. Precipitation shows no clear directional trend over the same period, with high interannual variability masking any potential systematic shift; a tentative recent drying signal is visible in the smoothed record but remains within the range of natural variability. These observed signals — unambiguous warming coupled with uncertain but potentially declining precipitation — are consistent with the projected future climate described in Section [X.4] and underpin the identification of heat stress, increased evapotranspiration demand, and reduced surface water availability as key climate hazards for the project infrastructure.
