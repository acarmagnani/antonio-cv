# Climate Hazard Assessment

## Hazard Identification

The climate and natural hazards potentially relevant to the project have been identified on the basis of: (i) the climate baseline and trend analysis presented in Sections [X.2]–[X.3]; (ii) the future climate projections presented in Section [X.4]; and (iii) supplementary hazard screening using the ThinkHazard! platform (World Bank / GFDRR), which provides standardised hazard classifications for the three geographic zones covered by the project: Baku/Absheron, Aran, and Guba-Khachmaz. The ThinkHazard! classifications reflect the current hazard level based on available global data and use a four-level scale: Very Low / Low / Medium / High.

An important note on the ThinkHazard! water scarcity classification: the Low rating for all three zones reflects the tool's probabilistic definition of drought (1% chance of drought in a 10-year period), which captures episodic drought events but does not represent the persistent semi-arid moisture deficit of the Absheron Peninsula or the projected intensification of the moisture balance deficit indicated by the SPEI analysis (Section [X.4.5]). Strong winds and dust storms are not classified in ThinkHazard! but represent a well-documented local phenomenon on the Absheron Peninsula (the Khazri north wind and Gilavar south wind), relevant particularly for open infrastructure such as the Samur–Absheron Canal and the WTP intake structures. Caspian Sea level fluctuation is a distinctive regional feature driven by a combination of climatic and non-climatic factors; its relevance is limited to components with proximity to the Caspian coastline.

## Hazard Assessment by Component

For each project component, the relevant hazards are assessed in terms of **hazard magnitude** (severity and likelihood based on Sections [X.2]–[X.4] and ThinkHazard!), **exposure** (the degree to which the component is physically located in an area affected by the hazard), and **vulnerability** (the susceptibility of the component to sustain damage or operational disruption when exposed to the hazard). These three factors combine to inform the risk ratings presented in Section [X.7].

### Jeyranbatan Water Treatment Plant

The Jeyranbatan Water Treatment Plant (WTP) is located on the Absheron Peninsula, approximately 25 km north-west of central Baku, at an inland site adjacent to the Jeyranbatan Reservoir. The complex comprises both older conventional treatment units (1960s–1970s) scheduled for reconstruction and a modern ultrafiltration plant (2015). The project involves the reconstruction and modernisation of the older treatment units within the existing site footprint.

**Extreme heat** is the most operationally significant climate hazard for the WTP. ThinkHazard! classifies extreme heat at Medium for Baku/Absheron (>25% chance of a heat stress period in the next 5 years), and the CMIP6 projections indicate that the frequency of days with Heat Index exceeding 35°C will increase from approximately 3 days/year in the historical baseline to 11–22 days/year by 2075 depending on scenario (Section [X.4.2]). The WTP is exposed to this hazard through multiple pathways: (i) elevated raw water temperatures in the Jeyranbatan Reservoir during summer, which affect coagulation kinetics and membrane performance; (ii) thermal stress on outdoor mechanical and electrical equipment; (iii) increased chemical oxygen demand and algal activity in the reservoir during prolonged heat events, raising treatment difficulty; and (iv) heat stress on outdoor operational staff. The plant's vulnerability is elevated by the age and condition of the existing conventional treatment units being rehabilitated, some of which were designed for the climatic conditions of the 1960s–1970s.

**Drought and reduced source water availability** pose a medium- to long-term operational risk to the WTP. The plant's primary source is the Jeyranbatan Reservoir, which receives water via the Samur–Absheron Canal (surface water) and to a lesser degree from local groundwater. The SPEI projections (Section [X.4.5]) indicate a progressive deterioration of the moisture balance for the Absheron region, with the ensemble median SPEI approaching −1.2 (moderate drought) under SSP2-4.5 by 2050. Reduced inflows to the Jeyranbatan Reservoir during drought periods would lower stored volumes, concentrate pollutants and nutrients, and increase turbidity during recovery rainfall events — all of which increase treatment demand and chemical costs. The WTP's exposure is high given its dependence on a single reservoir fed by a surface-water canal, and its vulnerability is elevated by the limited reservoir storage relative to seasonal demand patterns.

**Strong winds and dust storms** are a significant chronic hazard for the WTP at this location. The Absheron Peninsula experiences frequent and intense wind events, particularly the cold northerly Khazri wind which can carry substantial quantities of dust and particulates. Dust loading on intake screens and membrane filters increases maintenance frequency and reduces membrane operational life. The WTP's open reservoir is also vulnerable to wind-driven contamination. Vulnerability of the existing infrastructure to this hazard is assessed as medium, with the reconstruction works providing an opportunity to incorporate improved screening and enclosure of sensitive components.

**Extreme precipitation and river flooding** present a lower but non-negligible risk. ThinkHazard! classifies river flood as High for Baku/Absheron (expected at least once in 10 years), though the WTP site is inland and elevated relative to nearby drainage channels. The principal risk is from flash flooding of the site's surface drainage network during intense rainfall events, which could contaminate raw water intakes and flood chemical storage areas. As new construction under the reconstruction programme, the site drainage and chemical containment can be designed to current standards.

**Earthquake** risk is classified as Medium by ThinkHazard! (10% chance of damaging shaking in 50 years). The WTP's vulnerability to earthquake is moderate: the reconstruction will employ modern building standards, but existing infrastructure on site (pipelines, tanks, storage) may be vulnerable to differential settlement or joint failure. Chemical storage and associated spill containment are particularly sensitive to seismic events.

**Wildfire** risk is classified as High for Baku/Absheron, primarily due to dry summer vegetation conditions. For the WTP specifically, direct fire damage risk to the built infrastructure is low (concrete structures, limited combustibles within the plant footprint), but fire risk to access routes and to vegetation around the open reservoir and canal intake is relevant for operational continuity.


### Baku I and Baku II Transmission Pipelines

The Baku I (Şhollar–Baku) and Baku II pipelines are long-distance transmission pipelines transporting groundwater from springs and wellfields in northern Azerbaijan (Guba-Khachmaz zone) to Baku and settlements along the route. The pipelines traverse several hundred kilometres of varied terrain, including the Greater Caucasus piedmont, agricultural lowlands, and the northern approach to the Absheron Peninsula.

**Landslide** is the highest-severity climate-related hazard for the Baku I and Baku II pipelines, particularly for sections traversing the Greater Caucasus piedmont in the Guba-Khachmaz zone where ThinkHazard! classifies landslide susceptibility as **High**. The region's rainfall patterns, steep terrain, geology, and seismicity create conditions for frequent localised landslides. Pipeline sections at slope transitions, river terrace edges, and areas of known slope instability are exposed to soil movement that could damage or rupture the pipeline. The vulnerability of the existing pipelines (many sections dating from the Soviet era) is elevated by age-related deterioration of protective coatings and structural integrity, and by the absence of comprehensive slope stability monitoring along the entire route.

**Extreme precipitation and river flooding** present a significant hazard at the multiple river crossings along the pipeline routes. Both pipelines cross numerous rivers and streams on the way south from the Guba-Khachmaz zone, including crossings of seasonally significant watercourses. Scour of river banks at crossing points, undermining of pipeline supports, and debris impact during flood events are the primary damage mechanisms. Vulnerability is higher at crossings where the pipelines are supported above water level or where bank protection has deteriorated.

**Earthquake** risk is classified as Medium in the Guba-Khachmaz zone (10% chance of damaging shaking in 50 years). Long buried pipelines are generally less vulnerable to seismic ground shaking than above-ground structures, but are susceptible to permanent ground deformation at fault crossings, landslide zones, and soil liquefaction sites. The pipeline alignments may pass through or near the Aran zone, where earthquake hazard is classified as **High** (>20% chance of damaging shaking in 50 years), increasing the seismic exposure of the more southerly pipeline sections.

**Extreme heat** presents a secondary hazard for the Baku I and II pipelines. Buried sections are largely insulated from surface temperature extremes, but any above-ground sections, valve chambers, and pump stations are exposed to thermal expansion effects and equipment stress during prolonged high-temperature periods. The operational context is also relevant: during summer drought conditions, increased water demand in Baku places higher operational pressure on the pipelines, increasing the risk of failure at joints and weakened sections.

**Wildfire** risk is classified as High across the Guba-Khachmaz zone. Direct damage to buried pipelines from wildfire is limited, but above-ground components (valve stations, inspection chambers, surface markers), access tracks for maintenance, and cathodic protection equipment are potentially affected.


### Saray–Sharg Main Pipeline

The Saray–Sharg pipeline is a new-construction main pipeline connecting existing reservoirs on the Absheron Peninsula, improving system resilience and conveyance capacity within the Greater Baku distribution network. As a new-build project, it has the advantage of being designed and constructed to current engineering and climate-resilience standards.

The hazard profile of the Saray–Sharg pipeline largely mirrors that of the Absheron/Baku zone. **Extreme precipitation and river flooding** (High for Baku per ThinkHazard!) is the primary climate hazard, particularly at any watercourse crossings along the pipeline route. **Earthquake** risk (Medium) should be incorporated into structural design. **Extreme heat** (Medium) has limited direct relevance for a buried pipeline but is relevant for above-ground components and for operational demand management. **Wildfire** (High) affects access and above-ground components. **Landslide** risk is Low for the flat Absheron terrain. As a new construction, vulnerability to all these hazards can be minimised through appropriate alignment selection, design standards, and construction quality.


### Oghuz–Gabala–Baku Pipeline

The Oghuz–Gabala–Baku (OGB) pipeline is a long-distance gravity pipeline commissioned in 2010, transporting high-quality groundwater from the Gabala and Oghuz districts in western Azerbaijan to Baku. Its route traverses the Aran lowlands (Kura–Araz depression) — the zone with the most severe combined hazard profile across the three project areas.

**River flooding** is the most acute hazard for the OGB pipeline. ThinkHazard! classifies both river flood and urban flood as **High** for the Aran zone, meaning potentially damaging floods are expected at least once in the next 10 years. The pipeline route crosses the Kura River and multiple tributaries of the Kura-Araz system, which are prone to seasonal flooding and episodic high-flow events. Flooding at river crossings poses risks of scour beneath the pipeline, buoyancy uplift in buried sections in saturated soils, and damage to crossing support structures. Climate change projections for the region indicate that model projections of future river flows are inconsistent, but the present high hazard level may increase. The OGB pipeline's exposure is high due to its long traversal of the Kura-Araz floodplain, and vulnerability is elevated in sections where protective measures at crossings have deteriorated since commissioning in 2010.

**Earthquake** presents a very significant hazard for the OGB pipeline. The Aran zone is classified as **High** seismic hazard by ThinkHazard! (>20% chance of potentially-damaging shaking in 50 years), considerably higher than the Medium classification for the Absheron and Guba-Khachmaz zones. Long buried pipelines are susceptible to permanent ground deformation, fault displacement, and soil liquefaction during significant seismic events — all of which are mechanisms present in the Aran lowlands. Pipeline joints and connections at river crossings are particularly vulnerable. Earthquake damage to the OGB pipeline would interrupt a significant proportion of Baku's water supply from this source.

**Extreme heat** is classified as Medium across the Aran zone. The Aran lowlands are characterised by a hotter summer than the Absheron Peninsula, with mean July temperatures exceeding those of the coast. Above-ground pipeline sections and valve chambers are exposed to higher thermal loading, and peak summer operational demand increases hydraulic stress.

**Landslide** risk in the Aran lowlands is classified as Low by ThinkHazard!, consistent with the predominantly flat alluvial terrain. However, sections of the pipeline in the foothills zones approaching Gabala and Oghuz to the west may be exposed to higher slope instability, and this should be confirmed through route-specific geotechnical assessment.

**Volcanic hazard** is classified as Medium for the Aran zone by ThinkHazard!, reflecting proximity to mud volcano fields that are extensive and well-documented in the Kura-Araz depression of Azerbaijan. Mud volcano activity can cause localised ground deformation, gas emissions, and ejection of fluidised sediment that could affect buried pipeline sections in susceptible areas. While classical volcanic hazard (lava, pyroclastic flows) is not applicable, mud volcano ground deformation is a site-specific geohazard to be considered in detailed design and route alignment review.


### Samur–Absheron Canal

The Samur–Absheron Canal is an open canal conveying surface water from the Samur River (via the Tahtakorpu Reservoir) through the Guba-Khachmaz zone and across northern Azerbaijan to the Jeyranbatan Reservoir. It is the most climate-sensitive component in the project, as an open surface-water conveyance system wholly dependent on catchment precipitation and river flows that are directly affected by the climate changes projected in Section [X.4].

**Drought and reduced water availability** is the **highest-priority climate hazard** for the Samur–Absheron Canal and the most significant climate risk in the project overall. The canal's water source — the Samur River — is a glacier-fed and precipitation-dependent river whose flows are sensitive to both seasonal precipitation patterns and long-term changes in snowmelt timing and volume. The SPEI projections for the Absheron region (Figure [X].8) indicate a progressive deterioration in moisture balance approaching moderate drought conditions under SSP2-4.5 by 2050, with the 10th percentile of the model ensemble projecting severe drought (SPEI < −2.0) under SSP3-7.0 by 2075. Reduced inflows to the Tahtakorpu Reservoir directly reduce the volume available for conveyance through the canal. Compounding this, the canal currently operates with significant conveyance losses estimated from seepage through unlined or deteriorated sections, and these losses represent a further drain on available water during deficit periods. The canal's exposure to drought is Very High (it is a surface-water system with no alternative source), and its vulnerability is High (existing condition of lining and seepage losses amplify the effect of reduced inflows).

**Extreme heat and evaporation losses** represent an additional and growing hazard for the open canal. As an open-channel conveyance system, the canal loses water directly to evaporation from the water surface, with losses increasing as a function of both air temperature and wind speed. The projected increase in mean annual temperatures of +1.5–2.7°C by 2075 (Section [X.4.2]) will progressively increase open-water evaporation rates. Combined with the projected increase in hot days, this mechanism represents a physically certain (though difficult to precisely quantify) additional reduction in conveyance efficiency. The Absheron section of the canal, passing through the dry lowland zone exposed to the Khazri wind, is particularly susceptible.

**Landslide** risk is classified as **High** in the Guba-Khachmaz zone where the upper canal corridor is situated. Canal embankments in the piedmont section are exposed to slope instability driven by intense precipitation events, soil saturation, and seismic shaking. Embankment failure or landslide encroachment onto the canal corridor would disrupt conveyance and require emergency remediation. The vulnerability of existing embankment sections — some dating from the original Soviet-era construction — is elevated by age-related deterioration.

**Strong winds and dust** represent a locally significant chronic hazard for the canal sections crossing the Absheron Peninsula. The Khazri northerly wind is among the strongest persistent winds on the western Caspian coast, and can entrain significant quantities of dust and sand. In the open canal, wind-borne particulates increase turbidity in the conveyed water, adding to treatment load at the WTP. Canal bank erosion by wind action is also a recognised maintenance issue on the Absheron section.

**Extreme precipitation** can cause flash flooding of the canal corridor, particularly in the upper piedmont section where the catchment response to intense rainfall is rapid. Overtopping of canal embankments, sediment surges from eroded catchment slopes, and debris entrainment all reduce conveyance capacity and damage canal infrastructure. The vulnerability of the existing canal to these events is elevated by the condition of embankment and lining sections.

**Wildfire** risk is High across both the Guba-Khachmaz zone and the Absheron Peninsula. The principal risk for the canal is to riparian and embankment vegetation that stabilises canal banks; loss of this vegetation through fire increases erosion vulnerability. Access tracks for maintenance also pass through fire-prone areas.


## Preliminary Hazard Screening

On the basis of the zone-level ThinkHazard! classifications in Table [X].7 and the CCKNP climate projections in Section [X.4], each hazard–component combination is subject to a preliminary screening to determine whether it warrants detailed Exposure and Vulnerability assessment. The screening assigns a preliminary relevance rating (Very High / High / Medium / Low) reflecting the magnitude of the hazard at the component's location and the plausibility of a material impact pathway. Combinations rated **Low** — where the hazard is not applicable to the component's geographic situation, or the potential impact pathway is considered negligible given the nature of the infrastructure — are screened out and are not carried forward to the detailed assessment.
