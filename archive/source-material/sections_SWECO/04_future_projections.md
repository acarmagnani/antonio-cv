# Future Climate Conditions

This section presents the projected future climate for the Greater Baku region of Azerbaijan, based on global climate model outputs and emissions scenarios aligned with the latest scientific consensus. The analysis draws primarily on data from the World Bank Climate Change Knowledge Portal, which compiles information from the CMIP6 multi-model ensemble.

## Basis for Projections

The project comprises several infrastructure types with different expected lifespans:

- **Jeyranbatan Water Treatment Plant**: major civil infrastructure with a typical design life of 40–50 years; mechanical and electrical equipment [15–25 years].
- **Transmission pipelines** (Baku I & II, Oghuz–Gabala–Baku, Saray–Sharg): pipeline design horizon of approximately 50 years, though physical lifetimes may exceed 100 years.
- **Samur–Absheron Canal**: civil canal infrastructure with a long service life.

The analysis uses **2025**, **2050**, and **2075** as key time horizons, reflecting the expected construction, operational, and end-of-design-life phases of the project components. The 2025 horizon represents approximately the projected start of operations; 2050 corresponds to the mid-operational period for the major civil works; and 2075 represents the approach to the end of design life for the longest-lived components, including the civil structure of the WTP and the pipeline infrastructure. The long operational horizon of transmission pipelines (physical lifetimes potentially exceeding 100 years) and the canal civil works justifies the inclusion of a 2075 assessment horizon to capture longer-term climate change exposure.

The projections are based on CMIP6 using three Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) as described in Section [X.1]: SSP1-2.6 (near-term/low-emissions), SSP2-4.5 (intermediate emissions, applied to the mid-term horizon), and SSP3-7.0 (high-emissions reference scenario, applied to the long-term horizon). This three-scenario approach follows the JASPERS EC Climate Proofing Technical Guidance and is intended to ensure that the range of plausible future climate outcomes is adequately captured across the project's full operational horizon.

Mean annual surface air temperatures in the Absheron Peninsula are projected to increase progressively under all emissions scenarios throughout the assessment period. The warming signal is robust and consistent across all three SSP scenarios in the near-term (2025 horizon), where the committed warming already embedded in the climate system results in projected temperatures of approximately 14.8–15.3°C regardless of future emissions pathway — an increase of approximately 0.5–1.0°C above the 1991–2020 observed baseline of 14.3°C. Beyond 2025, the scenarios diverge: under SSP2-4.5, the ensemble median projects a mean temperature of approximately **15.8°C by 2050** and **16.3°C by 2075**, representing anomalies of +1.5°C and +2.0°C respectively above the CMIP6 historical baseline. Under the higher-emissions SSP3-7.0 scenario, warming accelerates more strongly in the latter part of the century, reaching approximately **17.0°C by 2075** (+2.7°C anomaly) — roughly twice the warming of the SSP1-2.6 trajectory at the same time horizon. The lower-emissions SSP1-2.6 scenario projects stabilisation at approximately +1.3–1.4°C above the baseline by mid-century, consistent with the global mitigation assumptions of this pathway.

The projected warming has direct operational implications for the project infrastructure. Increased mean temperatures drive higher evaporation losses from the open Samur–Absheron Canal and Jeyranbatan Reservoir, increase cooling water demand at the WTP, and reduce the performance of temperature-sensitive treatment processes. These effects are further amplified by the increasing frequency of extreme heat days discussed in the following sub-section.

| | CMIP6 hist. baseline (1995–2014) | SSP | 2025 | 2050 | 2075 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hot days (HI > 35°C) (days/year) | ~3 | SSP1-2.6 | 6 | 9 | 8 |

> Source: World Bank CCKNP, CMIP6 multi-model ensemble; model reference period 1995–2014. Values represent multi-model ensemble medians, rounded to the nearest whole day.


The number of days per year with a Heat Index exceeding 35°C is projected to increase substantially over the assessment period. During the CMIP6 model reference period (1995–2014), the ensemble median indicates approximately **3 days per year** with HI > 35°C for the Absheron Peninsula — consistent with the semi-arid climate characterised by hot but not extreme summers in the baseline period. By **2025**, the multi-model median projects approximately **5–6 days per year** across all scenarios, reflecting the committed near-term warming. By **2050**, the frequency increases to approximately **9–12 days per year** depending on scenario, with SSP2-4.5 projecting approximately 11 days and SSP3-7.0 approximately 12 days. The divergence between scenarios is most pronounced at the 2075 horizon: SSP1-2.6 projects stabilisation at approximately **8 days per year**, while SSP2-4.5 reaches approximately **15 days per year** and SSP3-7.0 reaches approximately **22 days per year** — representing roughly a seven-fold increase over the historical baseline under the high-emissions pathway.

The increasing frequency of heat stress days is particularly relevant for the Jeyranbatan WTP (worker safety and mechanical equipment performance), the Samur–Absheron Canal (enhanced evaporation losses during peak demand periods), and exposed pipeline sections (thermal expansion and soil heave in clay-rich materials).

Projected annual precipitation for the Absheron Peninsula shows no strong directional trend but exhibits marked interannual variability and increasing model uncertainty across the assessment period. The high variability in modelled precipitation — characteristic of semi-arid regions where precipitation is episodic and sensitive to synoptic weather patterns — means that projection uncertainty is inherently larger for precipitation than for temperature.

Under **SSP2-4.5**, the multi-model ensemble median projects modest decreases in annual precipitation relative to the CMIP6 historical baseline (~357 mm), with anomalies of approximately −3 mm at 2025, −9 mm at 2050, and −2 mm at 2075. While these anomalies are small in absolute terms, the systematic direction of slightly decreasing precipitation under the intermediate scenario, combined with rising evapotranspiration driven by higher temperatures, implies a reduction in the effective moisture balance that is relevant for source water availability. Under **SSP3-7.0**, the projected anomalies exhibit greater inter-model spread and a less consistent directional signal, reflecting the challenges climate models face in projecting precipitation over the Caspian region at higher forcing levels. The **SSP1-2.6** scenario projects near-neutral to slightly positive precipitation anomalies at most horizons.

It should be noted that the CMIP6 modelled historical mean for this region (~357 mm/year) is systematically higher than the CRU TS v4.06 observed baseline (307 mm/year, 1991–2020). This bias is documented for the Absheron Peninsula in the CCKNP data and is attributable to the coarse spatial resolution of CMIP6 models. All projected values and anomalies in Table [X].3 are therefore expressed relative to the CMIP6 modelled baseline; the direction and relative magnitude of changes are the more informative quantities rather than the absolute projected values.

Taken together, the projections indicate that the Absheron Peninsula will experience a progressive intensification of its semi-arid climate characteristics over the assessment period: rising mean temperatures, more frequent heat extremes, and a modest long-term reduction in available precipitation relative to evapotranspiration demand. The near-term (2025) projections are broadly consistent across scenarios, reflecting committed warming; the longer-term (2075) projections under SSP3-7.0 represent significantly more challenging conditions that should inform worst-case scenario planning for the infrastructure components with the longest design lives.

The projected decrease in frost days — from approximately 32 days per year in the historical baseline to 12–19 days per year by 2075 depending on scenario — represents a secondary climate signal of relevance to the project. Reduced frost frequency diminishes the risk of pipe freezing and ground frost heave in shallow pipeline sections during winter months, representing a modest climate benefit for the infrastructure. However, this benefit is secondary in magnitude to the risks posed by increasing heat, reduced moisture balance, and the intensification of drought conditions described below.

## Drought Outlook

Figure [X].8 presents the projected Standardised Precipitation-Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI) for the Absheron Peninsula. The SPEI integrates both precipitation and temperature-driven evapotranspiration into a single moisture balance indicator, making it particularly informative for semi-arid regions where drought risk is driven as much by increasing evaporative demand as by declining rainfall. A SPEI value of 0 corresponds to the long-term average moisture balance; values between −0.5 and −1.0 indicate mild drought, −1.0 to −1.5 moderate drought, and below −1.5 severe drought conditions.


**Figure [X].8:** Projected SPEI Drought Index; Absheron Peninsula, Azerbaijan

![Projected SPEI drought index, Absheron](data/graphs/Absheron/Projected drought Absheron.jpeg)


During the CMIP6 model reference period (1995–2014), the ensemble median SPEI for the Absheron Peninsula fluctuates around approximately −0.2, consistent with the region's established semi-arid character. The projections indicate a progressive deterioration in moisture balance across all scenarios from 2015 onwards. By **2025**, the multi-model median SPEI reaches approximately −0.6 to −0.8 (mild drought range) under all three SSPs, reflecting committed near-term warming and its effect on evapotranspiration. By **2050**, the SSP2-4.5 scenario projects a median SPEI of approximately **−1.2**, crossing the moderate drought threshold, while SSP3-7.0 reaches approximately −0.9 (upper mild drought). By **2075**, the deterioration continues: the SSP3-7.0 ensemble median reaches approximately **−1.6** (moderate to severe drought), and the 10th percentile of the model ensemble reaches −2.8 (severe drought) under the same scenario, indicating that a substantial fraction of CMIP6 models project severe drought conditions in the region by the end of the century.

The consistently negative SPEI trajectory has direct implications for the project's water source components — particularly the Samur–Absheron Canal, which depends on surface water from the Samur River catchment, and the Jeyranbatan Reservoir, which receives water via the canal. The SPEI signal is driven primarily by rising temperatures increasing potential evapotranspiration rather than by consistent precipitation decline, meaning that even if precipitation is maintained at current levels, effective moisture availability for source water recharge will decrease. This is the most significant long-term climate signal for the project and is examined in detail in the hazard assessment (Section [X.6]).
