# Reference letters, the general strategy

The rules that apply to every reference letter, whoever signs it. The per-person files in `reference_letters/<person>/strategy.md` hold only what is specific to that referee: who they are, what we did together, which facts are confirmed, their feedback on me, and which projects go in which paragraph. Everything below is assumed there and is not repeated.

## What these are for

A reference letter here is evidence for a job family, not a character reference. It is drafted by me and sent to the referee to sign as it stands, or to edit as they see fit. That is the offer I make when I ask, and it is why the drafting bar is high: nothing goes into a letter that I cannot defend in an interview, and nothing is attributed to the referee that they did not actually supervise.

## One referee, one umbrella

I am applying across several job families (real estate, ESG and sustainability consulting, data and computational roles, infrastructure), and no single referee has seen all of it. So each referee covers ONE umbrella: the job family the work we actually did together is evidence for. A second referee covers a different one, and between them the set covers the search.

**The umbrella is stated explicitly in the first paragraph of every letter**, not left to be inferred from the examples. A reader who skims one paragraph should come away with the category, and a referee who is asked to write "something specific to what you are applying for" is being given exactly that.

The umbrellas as they stand:

- **Tereza Kramlova (Ramboll):** consulting, ESG and sustainability, data, supply chain, innovation.
- **Sweco referees (Lukas Friga, Emma, Kirsten):** internationally financed infrastructure, climate risk, GIS and spatial data, tenders and proposals. To be split between them once they answer, since they saw different halves of that work.
- **Joachim (Ramboll):** not yet asked. His umbrella is decided partly by what Tereza's cannot carry: the Critical Raw Materials study under the EU Critical Raw Materials Act was his, so supply chain mapping, regulatory drivers and the business development side sit with him.
- **Jonathan Martins (Ramboll), worth asking:** Global Industry Lead for Buildings & Cities, MRICS and Board Chair of RICS Denmark, and the person the real estate ESG due diligence service line was built with (`reference/people/jonathan-martins.md`). That makes him the natural referee for the **real estate** umbrella, which is the destination and which none of the current referees covers, and a letter about real estate ESG work signed by an MRICS who chairs the RICS board in Denmark is the strongest signature available in this whole set.

## Shape of the letter

Four paragraphs, roughly one page.

1. **Opening.** Who the referee is, their title and organisation, in what capacity they supervised me and over what period, and the explicit umbrella statement. It also carries a general description of the territory, naming the frameworks, standards or regulation the work sat inside, so that the examples which follow read as illustrations of a wider body of work rather than as the whole of it. Naming a framework as territory I worked in is not the same as claiming a deliverable under it, and the letter has to keep that line visible.
2. **and 3., the two project paragraphs.** Each one built the same way: what the work was, what I did in it, what it was useful for, and what it meant in the end. The example has to be concrete (named client where the referee is comfortable naming it, named frameworks, real mechanics), because the concreteness is the whole difference between a letter that carries weight and one that reads as a formality. Their job is to demonstrate the qualities, not to inventory the work.
4. **Closing.** The overall judgement: how I worked with the team and with clients, and the qualities the referee actually named when they gave me feedback, ending in a plain recommendation for the kind of role the first paragraph identified.

## Written for the postings, not in general

Every per-person `strategy.md` carries a section reading across the applications in that referee's umbrella: what those postings actually ask for, which keywords and hard skills recur, which of them the shared work honestly evidences, and which of them the letter must not claim. The letter is then written in the vocabulary those postings use, wherever the vocabulary is genuinely earned. This is what Tereza meant by "as specific as possible to what you are applying for", and it is the difference between a letter that a screener recognises as relevant and one that reads as generic praise.

Two sources feed it, and both should be checked before drafting. First, `content_base.yaml`: the referee's block there often holds work this brief forgot, and a bullet good enough for the CV is usually good enough for the letter. Second, the referee's own LinkedIn or CV: where their description of their role overlaps with work I did under them, borrowing their vocabulary makes the letter sound like them, which is exactly the point. Borrow only where the overlap is real, since lifting a senior person's whole profile into a junior's record is the most conspicuous way to get caught.

## Truthfulness, which is stricter here than anywhere else in this repo

Someone else signs this, so the constraints are tighter than for my own CV or cover letter.

- **Nothing the referee did not see.** If they were not on a project, it is not written in their first person. Where a project belongs to a colleague and the referee sat above it, the letter says so honestly rather than claiming direct supervision.
- **Attribution is exact.** Work I built alone is written as mine; work I shared is written as work I carried a large part of, never as something I invented. Overclaiming in a letter I drafted for someone else to sign is the single worst failure mode available here.
- **No invented numbers.** No hours saved, no percentages, no efficiency figures unless the number is real and I can source it. Qualitative outcomes are fine and often stronger: manual reconsolidation eliminated, an assessment that stays current instead of being rebuilt at each revision, a client able to see where its exposure actually sits.
- **Nothing that did not ship, as a deliverable.** Work that was built but never used cannot be presented as something the client received. It can appear as evidence of initiative or range, abstracted and without the client's name, as long as the sentence stays true whether or not the thing went live. That distinction is worth keeping, because a concrete instance of proposing something nobody asked for is worth far more than the word proactive in a closing paragraph.
- **Details only where they are certain.** Where my memory of a client's specifics is approximate, the letter uses the wider true statement rather than the precise guess. A referee should never be put in the position of having signed something a client could correct.

## Voice

- **English, first person, in the referee's voice**, formal but not stiff. It has to sound like a senior professional writing about someone they managed, not like a template.
- **Long, articulated, connected sentences.** No short punchy sentences used for impact, and no one-line paragraph beats. I dislike that register, it reads as artificial and machine-written, and it is the fastest way to make a letter look drafted by someone other than the person signing it.
- **No em dashes.** Commas, periods or parentheses.
- **Nothing self-diminishing.** No hedging about what I was still learning, no framing of the internship as a first step or a transition. The letter is a case for hiring me.
- **Every letter opens differently.** Three letters that all begin "I am writing in support of" tell a reader that one person wrote all three, which is exactly the impression that destroys their value. The opening should come from each referee's own vantage: how they know me, what they were doing at the time, what their department does. Vary the sentence structure of the closing recommendation for the same reason.
- **The closing recommendation stays inside what the referee actually knows.** Tereza can recommend me for ESG and sustainability consulting because that is her field. Emma cannot recommend me for real estate, and a project manager in an infrastructure consultancy listing real estate research and site due diligence as suitable seats reads as someone reciting a list handed to them, which is precisely the impression that ruins a letter. The umbrella governs which material goes into the body and which vocabulary the letter uses; the recommendation at the end describes the kind of work the referee saw and the standard it was produced to, and lets the reader draw the conclusion.
- **Formal register, and no colloquialism.** Not "struck by", not "chased", not "went to him constantly", not "ready to go in". A referee at this level writes in measured, declarative sentences. Tereza's letter is the calibration point for all of them: reread it before drafting the next one.
- **Detail earns its place by answering something a posting asks for.** A technical particular that matches a requirement is evidence; the same level of particularity about something no employer asked about is filler, and it dilutes the sentences around it. When in doubt, check it against the postings section before it goes in.
- **Be specific about terms, not about process.** The specificity that earns its place is in the vocabulary: the named method, the named standard, the word a practitioner would use. The specificity that does not is procedural detail, which colleague was on the project, that a book was lent, the physics of why a pipe silts up. Name the discipline correctly and describe what was produced; do not narrate the steps.
- **Write so that the work reads as larger than the sentence describing it.** Naming the components of a piece of work compactly (the trends, the exposure of each component, the matrix, the measures that followed) tells a reader in the field exactly what was done and implies the rest. Narrating the process step by step has the opposite effect: it makes the whole job look like the three steps that fitted on the page.
- **Never put the referee's own insight in my mouth.** If they were the one who explained why a system fails that way, the letter says they gave guidance and I took it up, which is true and is also the more attractive quality.
- **Editable without restructuring.** Any single sentence should be cuttable or softenable by the referee without the paragraph collapsing, because a referee who edits should not have to rebuild the thing.

## Process

1. Ask (LinkedIn or email, drafts in `notes/todos.md`), and offer to write a base version they can sign or edit.
2. They accept, and their LinkedIn PDF or CV goes in `reference_letters/<person>/` so the titles and dates in the opening paragraph are exactly right.
3. Write `reference_letters/<person>/strategy.md`: who they are, what we did together in full detail, the cross-check against `content_base.yaml`, the postings section above, what is confirmed and what is not, their feedback on me, and the per-paragraph plan.
4. Draft `reference_letters/<person>/reference_letter.md` using only what serves the umbrella.
5. Render it for sending: `python system/make_reference_docx.py reference_letters/<person>` writes `reference_letter.docx` next to the `.md`, in Word format so the referee can edit it directly. The `.md` is the source of truth and the `.docx` is generated, so edits go in the `.md` and the script runs again. Formatting mirrors the cover letters (Times New Roman, 11pt, 25mm top margin and 20mm elsewhere), with tighter line spacing because a reference letter carries more text, and **it has to fit one page**, which is a real constraint on how much detail survives.
6. Send, saying plainly that it is a starting point and that they should change anything they do not recognise as their own view.

## Format, which is fixed and should not be re-decided per letter

Set in `system/make_reference_docx.py`, mirroring the cover letters so the whole pack looks like it came from one person.

- **Times New Roman throughout.** Body 11pt, the letterhead name 14pt bold, and 9.5pt for a letterhead block that runs to three lines or more.
- **A4, margins 25mm at the top and 20mm on the other three sides**, identical to `system/assets/cover-letter.css`.
- **Line spacing 13pt, which is single spacing at 11pt, with 5pt between paragraphs.** Deliberately tighter than the cover letters' 1.5, which is generous because a cover letter is 500 words. A reference letter carries closer to 700 and at 1.5 it spills; 13pt is a normal business-letter setting and does not read as cramped.
- **One page, and this is a hard constraint rather than a preference.** It is what decides how much detail survives, so write the letter knowing that the third and fourth examples will not fit. The renderer checks it and warns when the letter spills.

## The two commands

- `python system/make_reference_docx.py reference_letters/<person>` renders `reference_letter.md` to `reference_letter.docx` and reports whether it still fits one page.
- `python system/make_reference_docx.py reference_letters/<person> --from-docx` does the reverse, rewriting the `.md` from the `.docx`. **Run this after any hand-editing in Word**, by Antonio or by the referee, or the next render silently throws those edits away. Word is where a letter like this actually gets edited, so the round trip is part of the workflow rather than an escape hatch.

## What goes on the page, since the conventions are not obvious

- **Letterhead:** the referee's name, their current title and organisation, and a contact line. The current title is what gives the letter its authority to a stranger reading it.
- **Date**, left as a placeholder for them to fill in on the day they sign.
- **"To whom it may concern"**, because the letter is for a search rather than for one employer.
- **First paragraph identifies the relationship**: who they are now, what they were then, in what capacity they supervised me, and over exactly which months. A reference that never says how the writer knows the candidate is worth very little.
- **Both titles appear**, and they do different jobs: the current one establishes standing, the one they held at the time establishes that they were in a position to observe the work.
- **Close with a recommendation and an offer to be contacted**, then the sign-off and their name. Placeholders stay in square brackets so they are impossible to miss and easy to delete.
- **Nothing implies their current employer endorses me.** If a referee would rather be explicit about that, adding "in a personal capacity" after their title solves it, and that is their call rather than mine.
