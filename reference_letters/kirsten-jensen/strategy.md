## What we actually did together

### 1. The Mongolia drainage review, which is how she met my work

A colleague, Júlio, was reviewing the technical design submitted by the engineering team on a project in Mongolia, an urban drainage scheme of open concrete channels running alongside the streets and passing under them at crossings, and I was given the task of checking whether the submitted design met the applicable Mongolian and international standards. I am not an engineer, so I taught myself what the check required: I read the drawings and schedules, worked out how the capacity of an open channel is calculated, and built a large spreadsheet covering every reach of the scheme, from TK0 to TK9, holding the geometry of each one, whether the section was rectangular or trapezoidal, the material, the slope and the resulting capacity, with the formulas set up so that each reach could be tested against the required minimum and the non-compliant ones flagged.

**The terminology, which the letter has to use correctly, because she taught this subject.** What I performed is a **hydraulic capacity check on open channel flow**, and the standard method is **Manning's equation**, in which the flow a channel can carry follows from its **cross-sectional geometry**, its **longitudinal slope** and the **roughness coefficient of the material**, usually written as Manning's n. That is the coefficient I could not name. Two failure modes matter and both are in what I described: capacity below the design flow, which means the channel overtops, and velocity below the **minimum self-cleansing velocity**, which allows sediment to settle and eventually blocks the channel. That second one is precisely the risk I identified where a section narrowed at a junction, and naming it correctly is what turns my account into an engineer's account.

I took the work to Kirsten because she had previously been responsible for part of that project. Her first reaction was to say that she had not known I was that technical, and that what I had done made sense. She then gave me guidance on what to watch for in that kind of system and lent me a book on water engineering, which I read and which improved the rest of the work.

**This is the strongest paragraph available in any of the four letters**, because it is not a claim about my character or my diligence; it is a specialist in the exact subject reporting that an architect taught himself her method and got it right.

### 2. The Azerbaijan climate assessment, which I wrote in full

I wrote the complete climate assessment for the Baku water supply project, having previously written part of one on an earlier project with her. The work covered both directions that this kind of assessment has to cover: the project's effect on the climate, and the climate's effect on the project. I researched the country's climate, the projections and the trends, established what they implied for the scheme, and worked through the consequences for design and for operation, for instance that a rising frequency of extreme heat days bears on working conditions and on how the infrastructure has to be specified, or that increased rainfall bears on foundations and on the resilience of assets already showing damage. The assessment closed with a matrix rating each component of the project against each climate hazard, so that it was visible which components were most exposed to which hazards and where adaptation measures had to be concentrated.

**Terminology.** This is a **climate risk and vulnerability assessment**, carried out under **JASPERS and EBRD climate proofing guidance**, which is what `content_base.yaml` already records. The two directions have proper names and should be used: **mitigation** for the project's impact on the climate and **adaptation** for the climate's impact on the project, the latter being **physical climate risk**. The scoring runs on **hazard, exposure, sensitivity and vulnerability**, resolving into a **risk rating per component**, and what follows from it are **adaptation measures**. What I called environmental salience is the same severity logic I used at Ramboll, but here the correct vocabulary is climate risk rating rather than salience, and the letter must not import the human rights word into a climate framework.

**No invented figures.** I remember the shape of the findings, not the numbers, so the letter describes the kinds of conclusion the assessment reached and never states a temperature, a frequency or a percentage.

### 3. Spatial data and the field mission

I was responsible for the QGIS side of the Azerbaijan project: loading the datasets we received, going out to find the ones we lacked from open sources, including the elevation of each reservoir, and consolidating all of it into a single QGIS project shared with the whole team. When social and demographic data arrived for the provinces, I joined it to the provincial boundaries so that each administrative unit carried its own attributes and the analysis could be done spatially rather than by reading two documents side by side. The correct description of that is a **table join onto administrative geometries**, and it is worth stating, because it is the difference between producing a map and building a spatial dataset.

On the field mission across the Baku water supply system, she asked me personally to record what we saw, so I took the photographs and the notes at each site, whether a broken pipe, a source, or a treatment plant, and afterwards compiled everything into a single document organised by site, which the rest of the team could then write into. I also consolidated the photographs and data that everyone else had taken into a folder structure by location, so that the project had one record instead of several private ones.

She also asked me for QGIS maps from time to time, which I produced quickly and willingly. **She is one of the few people in that office who works in QGIS herself**, which means her assessment of that work is again expert rather than polite, and she taught me things in it that I did not know.

## The feedback problem, and how to handle it honestly

**She gave me no departure feedback**, because she was on holiday when my internship ended and I did not see her before I left. Emma's and Lukas's letters carry what colleagues said about me; **this one cannot borrow it**, because putting other people's assessments into her mouth is exactly the failure the general strategy forbids, and it is also the version most likely to be spotted.

What she can say is what she herself observed, and it is enough: that she had not expected an architect to be able to carry out that hydraulic review, and that it stood up; that she gave me a book and guidance and that I came back having used both; that she asked me for spatial work repeatedly and it came back quickly and correctly; and that she is in a position to judge that work because she does it herself. A closing paragraph built on first-hand observation is more credible than one built on reported praise, so the constraint improves the letter rather than weakening it.

## Shape, and the opening in particular

Four paragraphs, one page, and it will be tight, so the field mission is the first thing to compress if it does not fit.

1. **Opening, and it must differ from the other three.** Tereza's opens with "I am writing in support of", Emma's from having held the same role, Lukas's from the department he runs. Kirsten's should open from her own discipline: a hydraulic engineer who has taught urban drainage design, working on water and climate risk projects in developing countries, who first came across Antonio's work when he brought her a review of a drainage design he had taught himself to carry out. That establishes her authority and the surprise in a single move, and the umbrella statement follows from it.
2. **The Mongolia review**, with the hydraulics named correctly and ending on her judgement of it.
3. **The Baku climate risk and vulnerability assessment**, both directions, the component matrix, the adaptation measures, and the fact that I wrote the whole of it.
4. **The spatial work**, the shared QGIS dataset with joined administrative data and the field mission record, kept short.
5. **Closing**, built strictly on what she observed at first hand, and a recommendation that stays inside her world: climate risk and adaptation work, environmental assessment on infrastructure projects, and technical analytical work where someone has to learn an unfamiliar method and apply it correctly.
